1. Defining Stigma

1.1 What is Stigma?

Stigma concerning mental health can be difficult to define. Watch this TedTalk to gain an introduction to what stigma is, and how we can reduce the stigma surrounding mental illness.

Stigma in the Context of Mental Health

Stigma is one of the major barriers to accessing mental health treatment. It can impede social interaction, lower quality of life, interfere with social roles, and prevent timely access to treatment (Centre for Addictions and Mental Health, n.d.). This creates a cycle of disadvantage. Recently, anti-stigma efforts have increased in many countries, including many taking place in Canada.

1.2.1 Public Stigma

Public stigma is what most people think of when they say “stigma”. Public stigma highlights the views of the public towards mental illness or a specific condition.

  • Includes public stereotypes and prejudicial attitudes toward people with mental illness
  • Stems from a history of considering those suffering from mental illness to be “mad and to”taint” the family, since it was considered hereditary

This type of stigma can pose serious, negative implications to individuals struggling with their mental health. For example, a public stigma surrounding mental illness is that people with mental illness are dangerous, incompetent, unstable and may even be the one to blame for their disorder. Though this seems like just an idea, these public stereotypes can have serious consequences on the lives of people living with mental illnesses, and are the basis for many discriminatory practices.

As a result of these stereotypes, discrimination may take place in the form of employers refusing to hire individuals living with a mental illness, landlords refusing to rent to them, and even healthcare systems offering lower standards of care.

Stigma in Healthcare Settings

Mental healthcare providers are said to contribute largely to public stigma through their behaviours towards patients seeking help.

Individuals who seek services from mental health professionals often rate these providers as being among the most stigmatizing groups they encounter.

Among reports include being patronized, excluded from decisions on treatment, assumed to lack the capacity to be responsible for their lives, told their hope for recovery is limited, and told they will never work in regular careers, complete school, or even be a parent.

Stigma from Friends and Family

Friends and family are also reported sources of public stigma towards individuals living with mental illnesses.

Individuals with lived experience of mental illness report friends and family members views towards their loved one appeared to change upon the discovery of their mental illness. The majority of this change is often the result of social stigma. Friends and family may experience shame and/or fear that a scandal would occur if others find out that they are friends with/related to someone with a mental illness.

Typically, the more severe the illness, the stronger these feelings are. Additionally, feelings of embarrassment and shame are often accentuated in cases where the mental illness in question is addiction or substance use.

1.2.2 Self-Stigma

Self-stigma occurs when an individual internalizes the negative stereotypes associated with their mental illness. This means they begin to believe the negative social conceptions held by society surrounding their condition. Individuals experiencing a mental illness report experiencing stigma as even more limiting and devastating to their lives than their illness. The negative consequences of self-stigma are far reaching, including reductions in self-esteem and self-efficacy, leading to more negative thoughts and in some cases, even suicidal ideation.

Self-stigma occurs when an individual living with a mental illness has thoughts such as, “I am incompetent and incapable and it’s my fault I’m not getting better.”

Labeling Theory

Labeling theory is a perspective that goes hand-in-hand with self-stigma and has been proposed as an explanation for its occurrence. Labeling theory states that diagnosing an individual with mental illness places a label upon them and they feel they are associated with any societal implications of being mentally ill, such as being incapable or dangerous. Eventually, the feelings of bearing the burden of society’s negative stigma lead to an individual attempting different defensive behaviours to prevent society’s rejection and further judgement. These defensive behaviours may include hiding their treatment or withdrawing from it as well as disengaging in social interactions. These result in more unfortunate circumstances such as higher unemployment rates and reduced social relationships.

1.2.3 Structural Stigma

Structural stigma occurs in the context of mental health when laws, policies, and practices of institutions result in unfair treatment of those with mental illness. While it often occurs unknowingly (through implicit cognitive biases), it expresses the inequities embedded in the fabric of our social institutions, organizations, and our shared ways of thinking and acting. It is further manifested through cycles of blame, shame and mistrust for individuals who seek help for their mental illness or addiction.

Finding a way

This type of stigma is especially harmful to those with lived experience of mental illness. It can result in inequitable access (including housing, employment, etc.) and a lower quality of healthcare for these individuals, whether their concerns are related to physical health, mental health, and/or substance use.
